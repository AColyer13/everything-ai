AI Pioneers

The people most often called the founders, or "fathers", of artificial intelligence: Alan Turing, John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky and the three deep-learning researchers Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio and Yann LeCun. What each one did, and when.

About this page

"Father of AI" is an informal title and there is no official list. Turing, McCarthy and Minsky are the usual names for the field's founding era. Hinton, Bengio and LeCun are often called the "godfathers of deep learning". The people behind today's language models are on the LLM Pioneers page, and the full story is on History of AI. Dates come from public sources (Wikipedia and the people's own pages) and career details may lag behind recent moves.

Alan Turing

A British mathematician who asked, before computers existed in any practical form, what it means for a machine to compute and whether a machine could think.

What he did

  • The Turing machine (1936). A simple model of computation that defines what any computer can and cannot calculate.
  • "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950). Opened with the question "Can machines think?" and proposed the imitation game, now called the Turing test: if a person cannot tell a machine's written answers from a human's, the machine can fairly be called intelligent.
  • Codebreaking. Led work at Bletchley Park during the Second World War, which also shaped early computing.

He died in 1954, two years before the field was named, which is why he is called a founder rather than a participant.

John McCarthy

An American computer scientist who gave the field its name and a programming language to work in.

  • The name (1955 to 1956). Coined "artificial intelligence" in the proposal for the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, which is usually treated as the birth of AI as a field.
  • Lisp (1958). Created the language that dominated AI research for decades.
  • Labs and time-sharing. Co-founded the MIT AI project and later started the Stanford AI Laboratory (1963). Pushed early time-sharing computers.
  • Recognition. Won the Turing Award in 1971. Died in 2011.

Marvin Minsky

An American researcher who helped found the symbolic side of AI, and who is also remembered for a famous critique of neural networks.

  • SNARC (1951). Built an early machine that simulated a small neural network, as a graduate student.
  • MIT AI Lab (1959). Co-founded with John McCarthy.
  • Perceptrons (1969). Written with Seymour Papert. It showed what single-layer perceptrons cannot do, such as XOR, and is widely credited with cooling interest in neural networks. See the "XOR crisis" on History of AI.
  • Recognition. Won the Turing Award in 1969. Died in 2016.

Geoffrey Hinton

A British-Canadian researcher who kept working on neural networks through the years when they were out of fashion.

  • Backpropagation (1986). With David Rumelhart and Ronald Williams, published the paper that showed multi-layer networks can learn useful internal representations by passing errors backward.
  • Boltzmann machines (1985). Early generative neural models, developed with Terry Sejnowski and David Ackley.
  • AlexNet (2012). With students Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever, won the ImageNet competition by a wide margin using a deep network on GPUs, which started the deep-learning boom.
  • Google (2013 to 2023). Joined after Google acquired his company. In May 2023 he left so he could speak freely about AI risks.
  • Recognition. Shared the 2018 Turing Award with Bengio and LeCun, and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics with John Hopfield.

Yoshua Bengio

A French-Canadian researcher at the Université de Montréal and the founder of the Mila research institute.

  • Neural language models (2003). Early work showing a network could learn to predict the next word, an ancestor of today's LLMs.
  • Attention (2014). Co-authored the paper that introduced attention for translation, the idea the Transformer later built on.
  • Textbook and students. Co-wrote Deep Learning (2016) and advised Ian Goodfellow, who invented GANs.
  • AI safety. Launched the nonprofit LawZero in June 2025 to work on AI that is safe by design.
  • Recognition. Shared the 2018 Turing Award with Hinton and LeCun.

Yann LeCun

A French-American researcher known for the networks that made computers good at images.

  • Convolutional neural networks (late 1980s to 1990s). Developed CNNs and the LeNet family, which read handwritten digits for check processing. They are the ancestors of modern image models. See the CNN section on Deep Learning.
  • Meta. Founded Facebook AI Research (2013) and served as its chief AI scientist.
  • AMI Labs. Left Meta in 2025 and started AMI Labs (Advanced Machine Intelligence), a Paris-based startup working on "world models". He argues that language models alone are not the path to human-level AI. The company reported a seed round of about $1.03 billion in March 2026.
  • Recognition. Shared the 2018 Turing Award with Hinton and Bengio.

The common thread

The founders asked whether machines can think and what to build first. Turing posed the question, McCarthy and Minsky built the symbolic approach, and Hinton, Bengio and LeCun made the rival approach, learning from data with neural networks, work at scale. The history of AI is the story of those two ideas competing. The next generation, the people behind GANs and language models, is on LLM Pioneers.
